## [en] Belonging beyond the homeland

Florence Gurung’s Oxford thesis explores Gurung experiences of belonging in Britain, including settlement in Rushmoor, religious institutions and community identity. Its British setting is explicit; the findings are not assumed to describe every overseas Tamu community.

## [en] From barracks to settlement

Gurkha soldiers served Britain from the early nineteenth century, but for most of that time they were not welcome to settle in the country they had fought for. Veterans returned to Nepal, or retired to Hong Kong, Malaya or India, and only a small number obtained permission to remain in Britain. The 1997 handover of Hong Kong removed one of the main places where retired Gurkhas had built lives, and it pushed the question of settlement into public debate. Campaigners argued that men who had fought for Britain should be allowed to live there; the government resisted on grounds of cost and precedent. The eventual change came through legal cases, parliamentary pressure and a very public campaign.

## [en] Aldershot, Farnborough, Reading and Ashford

The towns that now have the largest Gurung populations grew around military bases and cheap housing. Aldershot, the home of the British Army, had an existing Gurkha presence; Farnborough, Reading, Ashford, Maidstone, Folkestone, Salisbury and Colchester attracted families through chain migration, as relatives and neighbours followed the first settlers. Community life organised quickly around rented halls, Lhosar committees, ex-Gurkha associations, shops selling Nepali goods, and later Buddhist centres and monasteries. A town that had no Nepali population in 1990 could have a Lhosar programme, a language class and a funeral society by 2010. These institutions are the infrastructure through which a dispersed community becomes visible.

## [en] Second and third generation

Children born in Britain grow up in a different relationship to Nepal from their parents. Many speak English at home and understand Tamu Kyi only partially, visit Nepal occasionally, and learn about clan and festival through family stories rather than daily practice. Some join the British Army, continuing a family tradition that is now a career choice rather than an obligation; others marry across communities and move away from the garrison towns. Community organisations worry about language loss and about the thinning of ritual knowledge, and they respond with Saturday classes, dance groups and youth wings. The second generation is also the first to produce its own writers, artists and researchers, who describe Gurung life in Britain from the inside.

## [en] Campaigns, recognition and what remains

The Gurkha justice campaign achieved settlement rights in 2009, and later decisions extended equal pensions and citizenship to more veterans. Memorials, commemorations and public recognition followed, including a national Gurkha memorial in London. Yet the campaign also revealed how much had been left undone: widows who never received a pension, veterans who died before the rules changed, families separated by paperwork, and a younger generation still asked to explain why their grandfathers were not treated as equals. The Gurung community in Britain is now part of the wider Nepali diaspora as well as a Gurkha community, and its politics reflect both inheritances.
