## [en] Belonging beyond the homeland

Florence Gurung's Oxford thesis explores Gurung experiences of belonging in Britain, including settlement in Rushmoor, religious institutions and community identity. It is the kind of study that begins with a question a community asks about itself, and it answers it with evidence from one place.

Its British setting is explicit, and its findings are not assumed to describe every overseas Tamu community. Rushmoor is a garrison district; Hong Kong was a garrison colony; Sikkim was a kingdom with a Gurung population older than any British connection. A study of belonging in one of those places is a study of that place.

How those communities came to exist is a story of soldiers and policy, and it is worth telling in that order, because the policy came second.

For most of the connection's history, the men who served Britain were not expected to live in it, and the towns that now hold the largest Gurung populations were built by a decision that came very late.

That decision, and the campaign that forced it, is where the British story begins.

A thesis is also a reminder that the community has been studied by its own members, which changes what counts as a source: a researcher who can be corrected by her grandmother writes differently from one who cannot.

Belonging is the right question for a study of this kind, because it can be answered by the people who hold it — in what they join, what they attend and what they teach their children — rather than only by a form.

## [en] Military and family migration

Pariyar connects settlement-policy changes with the growth of veterans' communities and examines the social bonds and exclusions that can accompany them — context for military-linked migration without reducing the entire diaspora to service personnel.

The distinction matters because the British Gurung population is often described as if every family arrived through the army. Many did; others came as students, nurses, chefs, students who stayed, and relatives who joined a household already here. The army explains the geography, not every biography.

The exclusions Pariyar examines are part of the same picture. A community formed around a shared regimental past can be warm to its own and hard on newcomers, and the bonds that make a Lhosar committee effective can also decide who is not invited to join it.

None of this is peculiar to Gurungs, and that is the point of treating migration as policy rather than as adventure. People went where they were permitted to go, and the shape of the community follows the permission.

But a community is not only the sum of its permissions, which is what the next section is about.

The bonds and the exclusions often have the same cause. An association built to help veterans' widows may be very good at that and very poor at welcoming a family that arrived through a different route entirely.

Settlement policy is also the reason the community's map looks the way it does: towns near bases, flats near a station, a temple in a converted building on a road that was cheap because nobody else wanted it.

## [en] A living, varied community

People preserve and reinterpret heritage through family life, organisations and religious practice.

That sentence is deliberately plain, because the alternative is a list of customs presented as a checklist of authenticity. In practice a family keeps what it can use: a festival that fits the school calendar, a language class that fits a Saturday, a grandmother's recipe that survives because a child likes it. What is kept changes, and the changing is not decline.

No population estimate appears here without a defined source, year and denominator, and other country profiles will be added when adequate Gurung-specific documentation is available. That rule exists because the numbers circulating about the British community are frequently invented, and an invented number is worse than an admitted gap.

The community is also varied in a way that any single description flattens: soldiers' descendants, restaurateurs, nurses, students, second-generation professionals, recent arrivals from Nepal, and families who have been in Britain for three generations and no longer speak Nepali at home.

What holds them together is not a shared experience but a shared name and a set of occasions.

The largest of those occasions began with a war.

A country profile is a promise about evidence: it says what is known, by whom and for which year, and it stops at the edge of what can be checked rather than filling the space with a round number.

Reinterpretation is visible in the calendar. A Lhosar that falls on a weekday is held on the nearest weekend, and the move is not a compromise with the tradition so much as the tradition being practised where its people now work.

## [en] From barracks to settlement

Gurkha soldiers served Britain from the early nineteenth century, but for most of that time they were not welcome to settle in the country they had fought for.

Veterans returned to Nepal, or retired to Hong Kong, Malaya or India, and only a small number obtained permission to remain in Britain. The 1997 handover of Hong Kong removed one of the main places where retired Gurkhas had built lives, and it pushed the question of settlement into public debate.

Campaigners argued that men who had fought for Britain should be allowed to live there; the government resisted on grounds of cost and precedent. The eventual change came through legal cases, parliamentary pressure and a very public campaign.

The sequence is worth remembering: the right was not given as a reward for service, it was won by people who had already served and then had to argue. A Joanna Lumley on a podium and a veteran with a file of papers are both part of how the policy moved.

What followed was not a community arriving in one place but a community forming in several.

The campaign also changed how the community describes itself. A generation that had thought of itself as guests began, quite quickly, to speak as citizens with a claim on the places it lived in.

The legal route matters for how the community understands itself: rights that were argued for are remembered as rights, while rights that were granted quietly are often forgotten by the grandchildren who hold them.

## [en] Aldershot, Farnborough, Reading and Ashford

The towns that now have the largest Gurung populations grew around military bases and cheap housing.

Aldershot, the home of the British Army, had an existing Gurkha presence; Farnborough, Reading, Ashford, Maidstone, Folkestone, Salisbury and Colchester attracted families through chain migration, as relatives and neighbours followed the first settlers. The pattern is familiar from migration everywhere: one household arrives, sends word, houses a cousin, and a street becomes a network.

Community life organised quickly around rented halls, Lhosar committees, ex-Gurkha associations, shops selling Nepali goods, and later Buddhist centres and monasteries. A town that had no Nepali population in 1990 could have a Lhosar programme, a language class and a funeral society by 2010.

These institutions are the infrastructure through which a dispersed community becomes visible. A hall booked for a Saturday is also a claim on the town's calendar, and a funeral society is an answer to the question every migrant family eventually asks: who will do this here?

Which is why the community's geography is measured in halls as much as in streets.

The halls are worth naming because they are where the community does its business: a booking, a rota, a collection for a funeral, a room hired for a language class that nobody is paid to teach.

Chain migration also explains the surnames in a school year group, and why a family in Ashford may have cousins in Farnborough, Reading and Aldershot without any of them having chosen to live near the others.

## [en] Second and third generation

Children born in Britain grow up in a different relationship to Nepal from their parents.

Many speak English at home and understand Tamu Kyi only partially, visit Nepal occasionally, and learn about clan and festival through family stories rather than daily practice. Their Nepal is a place of holidays and relatives, not of fields and obligations, and that is not a failure of upbringing; it is what distance does.

Some join the British Army, continuing a family tradition that is now a career choice rather than an obligation; others marry across communities and move away from the garrison towns. Both are ordinary outcomes, and both change what the community is.

Community organisations worry about language loss and about the thinning of ritual knowledge, and they respond with Saturday classes, dance groups and youth wings. The worry is well founded; the responses are uneven, because a class can teach letters faster than a family can teach a language.

The second generation is also the first to produce its own writers, artists and researchers, who describe Gurung life in Britain from the inside. That is a change in who gets to explain the community, and it is probably the most consequential one.

The campaigns that made the community possible are not finished either.

Language is the sharpest measure of the change. A grandchild who understands the scolding but answers in English is not outside the community; she is where the community currently is, and the classes exist because her parents know it.

The organisations are also where the generations negotiate. A youth wing that runs a dance group is teaching a repertoire and deciding, at the same time, which parts of it are worth an hour of a Saturday.

## [en] Campaigns, recognition and what remains

The Gurkha justice campaign achieved settlement rights in 2009, and later decisions extended equal pensions and citizenship to more veterans. Memorials, commemorations and public recognition followed, including a national Gurkha memorial in London.

Yet the campaign also revealed how much had been left undone: widows who never received a pension, veterans who died before the rules changed, families separated by paperwork, and a younger generation still asked to explain why their grandfathers were not treated as equals.

That unfinished quality is the honest note to end on. A right won in 2009 does not travel backwards, and a community that organised to win it learned things about itself that it now uses for other campaigns — language provision, religious sites, the teaching of Nepali in schools.

The Gurung community in Britain is now part of the wider Nepali diaspora as well as a Gurkha community, and its politics reflect both inheritances. A Lhosar in Aldershot is a Gurung occasion, a Nepali occasion and a British occasion at once, and the people in the hall know exactly which hat they are wearing.

The book ends where the community is: in several places at once, keeping a name.

The memorial in London is a national object, and the community's own commemorations are local ones: a plaque in a temple, a photograph in a hall, a programme printed every year with the same list of names growing shorter.

Recognition, in the end, is a local matter as much as a national one: a council that funds a language class, a school that marks Lhosar, a cemetery that allows the rites a family needs.
