## [en] Why it matters

When someone dies in a Gurung village, the question is not only where the body goes but where the soul goes. In Gurung belief a person's soul has to be guided on a journey to the realm of the ancestors after death, and a rite that is not performed properly is said to leave the soul wandering — and a wandering soul may bring trouble to the family that failed it.

That is the whole reason the rite exists, and it explains two things that otherwise look strange to an outsider: why families will spend money they do not have, and why they will wait months for the right person to conduct it. The rite is known as pae in Tamu Kyi and arghun or arghum in Nepali, and it is the largest ceremony a household will ever stage.

Everything about it follows from the belief. If the dead must arrive somewhere, then the living have an obligation that cannot be discharged by a quick prayer; if arrival depends on the route being recited correctly, then the person who knows the route cannot be replaced by a cousin with good intentions. The ceremony is large because the obligation is large, and it is long because the journey is long.

The first thing to establish, then, is who is qualified to lead it.

It is also the ceremony by which a household's standing is most publicly measured, because everyone who comes can see how the dead are being sent and how the living are behaving while they do it.

## [ne] किन महत्त्वपूर्ण छ

## [en] Who performs it

Ritual specialists such as the pachyu and the khlyepri (ghyabri) lead the ceremony with chants that describe the soul's path. The chant is a route: it names the places the soul must pass, the beings it will meet and the offerings that open each stage, and the specialist's authority rests on knowing it in the right order. A chant recited out of sequence is not a shortened rite; it is a different rite, and in the specialists' understanding it does not work.

In some places Buddhist lamas take part or perform the rite instead, and Stan Mumford describes how, in Manang, these practitioners guide the soul and even speak with it through possession. The coexistence is not confusion, and it should not be read as one tradition substituting for another. A household may hold a shamanic rite and a Buddhist one for the same death, because the two address different parts of the journey — the route out of the house and the country of the dead on the one hand, merit and rebirth on the other — and the family's obligation is to leave nothing undone.

That is the practical logic behind what looks like duplication. When the cost of failure is a wandering soul, a family does not ask which ritual is the correct one; it asks whether any has been left out. The answer, in many households, is that the shaman's chant and the lama's reading are not alternatives but two halves of a single duty.

Who exactly performs which part varies by place and by the specialists available, which is why the next section is about the gathering rather than about a fixed liturgy.

The drum carries the chant, and it also allows the audience to follow. Listeners who know the sequence can hear where the specialist has reached, and a mistake is audible to them before it is visible to a visitor.

A household with two specialists available will often invite both, one to chant the route and one to read, and the cost of doing so is understood as part of the duty rather than as extravagance.

## [ne] कसले गर्छ

पाच्यु र ख्ल्येप्री (घ्याब्री) जस्ता कर्मकाण्डी विशेषज्ञले आत्माको बाटो वर्णन गर्ने वाचनसहित विधि डोर्‍याउँछन्। कतै बौद्ध लामा सहभागी हुन्छन् वा विधि उनैले गर्छन्, र Stan Mumford ले मनाङमा यी विशेषज्ञले आत्मालाई डोर्‍याएर आवेशमा त्यससँग बोल्नेसम्म गरेको वर्णन गर्छन्।

## [en] A three-day gathering

Shanti Gurung describes the arghun as a three-day event that includes the syarga and klepri dances, performed in the name of the dead. Relatives travel from far away, bring offerings, and share food and labour, so the rite renews bonds between families as well as honouring the dead.

Picture the logistics of those three days. The gathering has to feed everyone who comes and house them, which in a village means borrowed rooms, borrowed pots and a great deal of rice; it has to keep the ritual sequence moving through the night, which means the specialists must be rested and relieved; and it has to keep the dancers going, because the dances are performed by people who have a relationship to the dead, not by hired performers. A nephew dances for his uncle, a daughter for her mother, and the obligation is part of the mourning rather than a performance added to it.

Three days is a minimum rather than a rule, and the length is read by everyone present. A funeral is therefore also a public accounting of who belongs to whom, and a family with many obligations will have a large one. Guests who come are making a statement about the dead and about themselves; guests who stay away are making one too, and both are remembered.

The presence of relatives from other villages, and now from other countries, is the point rather than a side effect. The rite that sends one person to the ancestors is also the occasion on which the living are counted.

Guests are also judges. A family that houses its visitors badly, or lets the dancing lapse at three in the morning, will be discussed for years, which is one reason the practical work of a funeral is taken as seriously as the ritual.

The dances are also where the young learn the steps they will one day perform for their own parents, which is why a funeral can look, at four in the morning, like a rehearsal as much as a rite.

## [en] A note on respect

The chants and objects used in pae are sacred knowledge held by trained specialists, and this page is a general introduction that does not reproduce ritual texts.

The restraint is deliberate, and it is not squeamishness about religion. A chant that guides a soul is not a poem to be anthologised; it is property, authority and, in the specialists' understanding, a working instrument. Publishing a version would create a text that looks authoritative and has never been performed — the worst of both worlds, since it would mislead readers and would take from the people whose livelihood and standing depend on keeping it.

There is a further reason, which has to do with what a text can carry. A route chant is tied to a landscape and to a lineage: the places named are places this family's dead must pass, and the sequence belongs to the specialist who learned it from his teacher. Printed without that context it becomes a list of names, and a list of names is not a rite.

What can be described is what the rite does, who stages it and what it costs, which is the rest of this chapter.

Consent here is not a formality. A specialist may permit a description and refuse a recording, and the two answers are consistent: one says what the rite is, the other hands over the instrument.

## [ne] सम्मानबारे एउटा टिप्पणी

प्येमा प्रयोग हुने वाचन र वस्तु तालिमप्राप्त विशेषज्ञले राख्ने पवित्र ज्ञान हुन्। यो पृष्ठ सामान्य परिचय मात्र हो र कर्मकाण्डी पाठ दोहोर्‍याउँदैन।

## [en] The funeral that lasts for days

A pae is not one ceremony but a sequence, and its length is a statement about the dead person and the household. The body is prepared and watched, kin are informed, and a specialist is called to establish what the dead require — which is not always known in advance, and which may differ from what the family expected.

Depending on the family's means and the specialist's reading, rites may run for a night, for several days, or be deferred for months until the harvest, a son's return from abroad or the right lunar day. Deferral is not neglect. A body may be buried or kept while the final rites wait, and the household lives in a state of suspended mourning until they are done; the wait is itself part of the ceremony's meaning, since it says that the dead are owed something that cannot be rushed.

Each stage has its own offerings, recitations and people: the pachyu or klepri who conducts, the lama where a Buddhist ritual is included, the women who lament, the affines who arrive with grain and money. The roles are not interchangeable, and a household that has the wrong person in the wrong place will hear about it.

The final rites release the dead into the ancestral line and release the household from mourning, and only then can ordinary life — marriage negotiations, house repairs, the next festival — properly resume. Until that moment a family is, in a real sense, paused.

The pause has practical consequences. Weddings are not arranged, houses are not repaired and festivals are kept quietly until the final rites are done, so a deferred pae can shape a family's calendar for a season.

The sequence also has a shape that visitors remember: the noisy nights, the quiet hour before the final release, and the moment when the household stops being a household in mourning and becomes an ordinary house again.

## [en] Who pays for a pae

A large funeral is expensive, and the cost is distributed in ways that record the family's place in a network. Sons and daughters contribute, affines bring grain, livestock and cash, village households send labour and food, and the specialist receives a fee that is often modest in cash but heavy in obligation — a debt of reciprocity that will be called in when his own family needs it.

Contributions are remembered, and a household that receives help at a death owes help at the next death in the giving family: a credit system without banks, kept in memory and enforced by reputation. A family that takes and does not give finds out, over years and at other people's funerals, what its standing actually is.

Migration has complicated the arithmetic. The person expected to pay may be in the Gulf or Britain and may return only for the final rites, sending money instead of presence, and the money is welcome while the absence is discussed. Funerals have therefore become both more expensive and more negotiated, and disagreements over who owes what are a familiar source of family tension.

None of this makes the rite less religious. It makes it what it has always been: a duty to the dead carried out through the obligations of the living, counted in grain and cash as well as in chants.

The account is kept by the women as often as by the men, since it is they who remember which household sent what, and a daughter who has married into another village may carry the ledger in her head for decades.

A funeral therefore redistributes a village's resources for a few days and then remembers the redistribution for years, which is a form of insurance older than any institution that would now be called one.
