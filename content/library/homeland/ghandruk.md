## [en] A place, not a universal model

Ghandruk sits on a ridge with Annapurna South filling the end of the street, and on a clear morning the mountain is so close that people stop noticing it. Tea houses stand where farmhouses stood. A stone staircase climbs between them, and somewhere below the last lodge a woman is turning maize on a mat, doing the work the village did before anyone came to look at it.

That double life is why Ghandruk is worth a chapter. It is not a typical Gurung village, and it is not a museum of one. It is a place where the things this book has described — clans, rituals, migration, soldiering — can be watched happening at once, in a village that has been photographed for a century and has opinions about being photographed.

Two things should be said before the studies are introduced. The village's landscape and architecture should not stand in for every Gurung village; community practices and opportunities differ across valleys and regions, and a village on a trekking route is a poor guide to one four days' walk from the nearest road. And a village studied by two researchers is not a village exhaustively known: it is a village known in two particular ways.

The walk in is part of the story. However a visitor arrives — on foot from the road below, or by the track that carries supplies — the last hour is a climb, and the village appears all at once, stacked against the mountain. That arrival has shaped what visitors think they are seeing: a place that looks like a postcard before anyone has spoken to a single resident.

The village also has the oldest kind of authority in the hills: elders who remember what the photographs do not show. Ask them what changed and the answer is rarely "tourism". It is usually about water, or wood, or the school, or which families left and which stayed. The mountain has been there the whole time; it is the arrangements around it that moved.

गाउँबारेका दुई अध्ययनले होसियार स्थानीय विवरणले कति बोक्न सक्छ देखाउँछन्।

## [en] Tourism and conservation

Adhikari and Fischer's 2011 study examines tourism, forestry and household livelihoods in two wards of Ghandruk.

It identifies benefits for livelihoods and conservation while acknowledging environmental and sociocultural trade-offs. Income from lodges and homestays reaches households that would otherwise depend on remittances and farming; the forest, once used freely, is now managed through committees and rules that came with conservation status; and the money is unevenly distributed, because a lodge on the main path earns more than a house at the top of the village.

The scope of that household research remains important when interpreting its conclusions. Two wards are not a district, and a good season is not a decade. A study carried out in a strong trekking year will report benefits that a weak year erodes, and a study of two wards will miss the households that left.

What the study shows, within those limits, is how a village negotiates a new economy without dissolving. That is more interesting than either of the stories usually told about trekking — that it saves a place, or that it ruins one — and it is harder to write, because the answer is that it does both, unevenly, over time.

Forestry is the part of the study that surprises readers who expect tourism to be the whole story. A village that cooks for trekkers needs fuel, and a village that was once free to cut is now bound by rules made in Kathmandu and enforced by a local committee. The committee is where the argument happens, and the argument is about who may take what from a slope that everyone can see.

The study's other finding is about conservation, and it is a finding about institutions rather than trees. A forest that is used by everyone and owned by the state is, in practice, used by whoever gets there first. A forest managed by a committee with a stake in next year's crop is used differently. The change was not in the resource but in who had the right to decide.

Adhikari र Fischer को सन् २०११ को अध्ययनले घान्द्रुकका दुई वडामा पर्यटन, वन र घरपरिवारको जीविका हेर्छ। यसले जीविका र संरक्षणमा हुने फाइदा देखाउँदै वातावरणीय तथा सामाजिक-सांस्कृतिक सम्झौतालाई स्वीकार पनि गर्छ, र त्यस घरपरिवार-अनुसन्धानको दायरा निष्कर्ष बुझ्दा महत्त्वपूर्ण रहन्छ: दुई वडा जिल्ला होइनन्, र एउटा राम्रो सिजन दशक होइन।

## [en] Culture in everyday life

Panta's 2023 study of Rodhi adds a locally grounded discussion of cultural transformation, and it is specific about where: Ghandruk, in Kaski.

Rodhi, the institution young people once used to meet, sing and court, has changed with the village. It is performed for visitors, remembered by elders, and sometimes revived by associations that want to keep the song tradition alive. The songs that were once sung at night in a house are now sung on a stage at a cultural programme, which changes both the audience and the reason for singing.

Read together, the two studies resist presenting tourism either as an uncomplicated rescue of heritage or as the sole cause of cultural change. Ghandruk's culture is not a display that tourism switched on, nor a victim it switched off; it is a working set of practices that people adapt as their livelihoods change. The adaptation is not evenly distributed either: a family that runs a lodge has a different relationship to performance from a family that farms and watches.

To see why, it helps to know what the village was before the lodges.

Rodhi is a good test case because it was never a performance in the first place. It was a room, a group of young people, songs and the ordinary business of meeting someone. Once it becomes a programme item, the question of what is being preserved — the songs, the sociability, or the memory of both — has no easy answer, and the village knows it.

What makes the Rodhi case worth dwelling on is that nobody planned the transformation. No committee decided that a courtship institution would become a stage item; it happened because the village's economy changed, the audiences changed, and the people who knew the songs found new reasons to sing them. Cultural change is usually like that — unplanned, uneven and argued about afterwards.

The two studies also disagree in emphasis, which is useful. The household study is interested in income and resources; the Rodhi study is interested in meaning and memory. A village read only through the first looks like a set of budgets, and a village read only through the second looks like a set of traditions. Ghandruk is both, and the two accounts sit beside each other here because neither is complete on its own.

Ghandruk is not the rule and not the exception. It is one village among many, changing at the speed of its visitors, and it is honest about the exchange.

## [en] Before the lodges

For most of its history Ghandruk was a Gurung farming and herding settlement on a ridge below Annapurna South: terraced fields, stone houses, communal labour and seasonal movement to the high pastures.

Men served in the Gurkha regiments and sent money home. Women managed households and fields, and took the decisions that a long absence made theirs by default. Trade routes connected the village to Pokhara and the north, and a man might carry goods for a season and return with cloth, salt and news.

The village's clans, its Rodhi, its festivals and its death rites structured social life, and the surrounding forest supplied fuel, fodder and building material. Labour was exchanged rather than hired, and a household's standing depended on how well it kept its obligations — to ancestors, to shrines, to neighbours. A family that failed at a funeral lost more than face; it lost the credit that would carry it through its own.

The photographs that made Ghandruk famous in the 1970s show a working village, not a heritage site. The houses are the same houses; the difference is that the people in the photograph were not performing for the camera, because there was no camera to perform for until quite recently.

The seasonal calendar is what a visitor never sees. In the months when the trail is quiet, the village returns to its older work: repairs, livestock, the fields, the funerals that were postponed until the harvest. A place that looks like a service economy for eight months of the year looks like a farming village for the other four.

The house itself records the economy. Stone below, timber above, a flat roof that dries grain, a hearth in the middle of the room and a store that holds a year of food: the architecture is a set of solutions to problems a lodge does not have. When a house becomes a tea house, the solutions are replaced by a menu.

A visitor who comes in October sees the village at its busiest and its most staged. A visitor who comes in July sees the fields, the mud and the work, and wonders why anyone photographs the other thing.

## [en] A village as a heritage brand

Tourism changed the village's economy and its self-image.

Lodges, tea houses and homestays replaced some farm work. The Annapurna Conservation Area brought rules, fees and committees, and with them a new kind of local politics: who sits on the committee, who collects the fee, who decides where a trail may go. The village became a stop on a route travelled by tens of thousands of trekkers each year, and the season set the rhythm of the year.

Ghandruk now presents its culture to visitors through museums, cultural programmes, dress and dance, and it benefits from the income and attention. The risk is that the version of Gurung life most visible to outsiders is the performed one, while the ordinary work of farming, herding and ritual continues behind it, less photographed and less understood.

That risk is not unique to Ghandruk, and it is not a reason to disapprove of the lodge owners who made the village prosperous. It is a reason to read the photographs carefully. The village is not a museum, even when it is displayed as one — and the next chapter visits a valley where the display is older, the mixture stranger, and the wealth came down from Tibet.

What the lodges did not change is the obligation. A death still requires the relatives, the specialist and the food; a marriage still requires the clans to agree. The village has added an economy without subtracting a system, and the tension between the two is what the two studies together describe.

The next village along the ridge has a different story, which is why one study cannot stand for a region. Some villages have more trekkers and fewer farmers; some have neither and are emptying; some have a road and a bus and a very different relationship to the path. Ghandruk is one answer to the question of what a Gurung village becomes, not the answer.

A reader who visits will see the lodges first and the obligations second, which is the wrong order. The right order is the one this chapter has followed: the work, the clans, the rites, and then the economy that arrived to look at them.
