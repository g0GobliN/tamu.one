## [en] The 1947 agreement

When India became independent in 1947, Britain, India and Nepal signed the Tripartite Agreement, which divided the ten Gurkha regiments. Four went to the British Army — the 2nd King Edward VII's Own Gurkha Rifles, the 6th Gurkha Rifles, the 7th Gurkha Rifles and the 10th Gurkha Rifles — and six stayed with the new Indian Army, where they became regiments such as the 1st, 3rd, 4th, 5th, 8th and 9th Gurkha Rifles. The agreement also set the terms under which men were recruited, paid and pensioned, and it created the system of British Gurkha depots in the hills that survived until the 1990s. Gurung soldiers served in both armies, and families often had brothers on either side of the divide.

## [en] Bases far from home

After 1947, British Gurkhas were based in Malaya, Borneo, Hong Kong, Brunei and later the UK, with the brigade's headquarters moving from Malaya to Hong Kong, where it stayed until the handover in 1997. Families sometimes lived with soldiers at these bases, and children grew up between Nepal and other countries: Nepali was spoken at home, English at school and Malay or Cantonese in the market. When the Hong Kong garrison closed, some families moved to Britain, some to Nepal and some stayed in Asia, and that dispersal produced the scattered communities that now organise themselves through associations and Lhosar events.

## [en] Unequal pay and pensions

For decades Gurkha soldiers received lower pay and pensions than British soldiers, and most could not settle in Britain after service. Veterans and their supporters campaigned against these differences from the 1990s.

दशकौँसम्म गोर्खा सिपाहीले बेलायती सिपाहीभन्दा कम तलब र पेन्सन पाए, र धेरैजसो सेवापछि बेलायतमा बस्न पाएनन्। भूतपूर्व सैनिक र तिनका समर्थकले सन् १९९० को दशकदेखि यी भिन्नताविरुद्ध अभियान चलाए।

## [en] The 2009 settlement decision

The campaign that changed this was built over two decades. Veterans' associations, the Gurkha Welfare Trust, British MPs and a public campaign led by the actress Joanna Lumley pressed the case that men who had fought for Britain should be allowed to live in it. In 2004 the government conceded a limited right of settlement for those who had served after 1997, and after a high-profile campaign and a court challenge it announced in May 2009 that most Gurkha veterans who had served before 1997 could settle in Britain. The decision brought thousands of families, many of them Gurung, to towns such as Aldershot, Reading, Ashford, Folkestone and Salisbury, where they built gurdwaras, churches, community halls, Lhosar committees and Saturday language schools. Mitra Pariyar's research looks at how these communities formed and at the inequalities inside them, including the position of Gurkha families who arrived without the savings or English that officers' families had.

## [en] Life after settlement

Settlement brought new chances but also hard questions: elderly veterans in a new country, language barriers, and how to keep Tamu traditions alive for children born in Britain. Community groups, Lhosar gatherings and language classes grew out of this new life.
