## [en] Gurung is not Gurkha

Gurung or Tamu describes a community identity. Gurkha belongs to a wider military history involving more than one community, and the two words sit next to each other so often in print that a reader can be forgiven for treating them as synonyms.

Macfarlane and Indrabahadur Gurung describe Gurungs as one of the major groups from which Gurkha soldiers were recruited, not the only group. Magars, Rais, Limbus, Tamangs and others served in the same regiments, wore the same uniform and are commemorated in the same histories, and a book about Gurungs that claimed the Gurkha story as its own would be taking other people's ancestors.

Getting that distinction right matters before any of the history is told, because almost every error in the popular account grows from it. If Gurung equals Gurkha, then a martial reputation becomes an ethnic description; if Gurkha means soldier, then service becomes the community's defining fact.

Neither follows. A community can be proud of a regiment without being reducible to it, and a regiment can be famous without its fame explaining the society that supplied it.

The history that follows is therefore about one community inside a military system, and the system was larger than any community in it.

The confusion is not a small one for a reader new to the subject. It decides whether the chapters that follow describe a people or a profession, and the rest of this chapter is written on the assumption that the answer is a people.

The word itself is a case in point: it entered English through the name of a place, Gorkha, and came to mean a kind of soldier, then a kind of man, and only then a subject of study.

## [en] Service and settlement

Pariyar's research examines military recruitment and the formation of Nepali Gurkha communities in Britain. Military institutions, caste relationships and migration policy all take part in that history, which cannot be told solely through battlefield reputation.

That sentence is a method as much as a summary. Recruitment was organised by institutions with paperwork; it interacted with caste ideas that determined who was thought suitable; and it ended, for many families, in a migration policy that decided whether a veteran could stay where he had served. None of those three is a story of courage, and all three shaped where Gurungs live today.

The result is a history that has to be told in two registers at once: the regiment and the household. A campaign is one kind of event; a recruiting officer's visit to a village is another; and the second determines who appears in the first.

For the families involved, the two registers were never separate. A man's service record and his family's land, marriage and standing were part of one calculation, which is why the paperwork matters as much as the medals.

What the archive can add to that is limited, and the limits should be stated.

Pariyar's point is structural rather than celebratory: the same system that produced a famous soldier also produced the paperwork, the pension office and the family waiting at home, and all of them belong in the account.

The archive's own limits follow from the same reasoning. A regimental history is good evidence about a regiment and weak evidence about a village, and the two have to be read as two.

## [en] Lives beyond service

Veterans, families and civilian communities have distinct experiences, and this archive will add biographies and military records only where identities and sources can be verified.

The caution is not bureaucratic. Service records are full of men with the same name from the same district, and a confident attribution that turns out to be wrong misleads a family about its own ancestor — a mistake that is much harder to undo than to avoid. Where a record cannot be tied to a person, it stays out.

The online version of A Guide to the Gurungs, for instance, is labelled a draft that differs from the published edition. That label is worth repeating, because the draft circulates freely and is often quoted as though it were the book. A reader who wants to cite it should know which version is in front of them.

The distinction between a veteran's experience and a civilian community's is also easy to blur. A garrison town's shopkeeper, a soldier's widow and a recruit's younger brother lived the same connection differently, and a history that speaks only of soldiers will misdescribe most of the people it claims to describe.

Before any of that, however, there were hill soldiers who had never heard the word Gurkha.

Civilian communities matter to this history because most Gurungs were civilians in every generation, including the generations when the regiments were busiest, and a history that counts only the men in uniform has counted a minority.

Verification means a name, a number, a district and a date that agree across more than one document, and where they do not agree the record waits until they do.

## [en] Before the Gurkhas: hill soldiers of the eighteenth century

The Gurkha story begins before the British. The army of Gorkha in the eighteenth century was built from the hill communities of its own territory, and Prithvi Narayan Shah's campaigns relied on soldiers raised through local chiefs and households.

Documentary research by Mahesh Chandra Regmi shows that the early Gorkha state recruited mainly Magars and Khasas, and that Gurungs from Lamjung entered the army more gradually, especially as the kingdom absorbed the territories in which they lived. The sequence matters: recruitment followed conquest and incorporation rather than preceding them, which means a Gurung soldier's service was often the last stage of a longer political process.

Service was tied to land, status and obligation rather than to a wage contract in the modern sense. A household supplied a man because it owed service, or because service protected what it held, and the return came as rights and standing as much as pay.

When the British later described Gurungs as a "martial race", they were codifying a relationship that had already existed between hill communities and states for generations. The phrase was new; the arrangement it named was not, and that is why it took hold so quickly on both sides.

The regiments that grew from it were built with extraordinary care.

The old obligations also explain why recruitment could be organised so quickly later: the household already knew how to supply a soldier, and the state already knew which households to ask.

A soldier's service could therefore be, at the same time, a duty owed to a chief, a claim on land and a route out of a valley, and the three explanations are not rivals.

## [en] The regiments and the recruiters

After the Anglo-Nepal War of 1814–16 and the Treaty of Sugauli, the British began recruiting Nepali soldiers, at first cautiously and then systematically.

The Gurkha regiments developed their own depots, recruiting officers, language training and regimental identities, and particular districts became associated with particular regiments. A young man from a certain valley might grow up knowing which regiment his father's village supplied, in the way another child knows which school is at the end of the road.

Recruiting officers travelled to the hills, examined recruits, recorded their caste and clan, and wrote the descriptions that later became the handbooks. Those handbooks are the origin of much of what is still repeated about Gurung character, and they were written by officers with a theory to prove.

The system was never simply military: it was an economic relationship, a diplomatic instrument and a racial theory at once. For a hill family, the recruiter's visit was an opportunity, a risk and a judgement about whether a son would return — and the family's answer was made with all three in mind.

Which is why the pensions that followed changed the hills more than the campaigns did.

The handbooks are therefore evidence of a relationship rather than a description of a people. They tell a reader what a recruiting officer expected to find, which is not the same thing as what was there.

The districts, the depots and the handbooks together produced a self-fulfilling description: an officer who expected to find certain qualities tended to write them down, and the next officer read the file first.

## [en] Pension, village and family

Military pay and pensions changed the material life of the hills more than any single development project.

A pension was a reliable cash income in an economy otherwise based on grain and barter; it paid for iron roofs, schools, land and ceremonies, and it gave retired soldiers a status that carried into village politics. A man who had been to Malaya came home with money, a language, a way of keeping accounts and a reputation, and villages learned to send their sons.

The absence of men for years at a time changed household labour, leaving women to manage farms and decisions, and it created a culture in which service was expected of sons. That expectation was not irrational: it was the most reliable route to cash a hill family had.

Regimental welfare funds, pensions offices and veterans' associations became part of village life, and the Gurkha connection produced both prosperity and dependence. The same income that built a school could make a village vulnerable to a change in policy decided far away.

That dependence is the part of the story that is hardest to celebrate.

The money also changed marriage, since a family with a pension could offer terms a family without one could not, and it changed housing, since an iron roof was a visible statement that someone in the house had served.

The pension also introduced a new kind of time into the hills, since a monthly payment is a rhythm that a harvest does not have, and households began to plan in years rather than seasons.

## [en] Memory and myth

The Gurkha story is genuinely important and genuinely partial. Victoria Crosses, campaign histories and regimental memorials record real courage, and families are right to be proud of them.

But the same story has been used to define Gurungs entirely as soldiers, to flatten a society of farmers, herders, weavers, priests and traders into a "martial race", and to make military service seem like the natural destiny of hill men rather than one option among several.

Recent scholarship, including Mitra Pariyar's work on caste, military and migration, treats the Gurkha connection as a social and economic system that shaped everything from marriage to housing. That framing does not diminish the courage; it explains the conditions in which the courage was asked for, and who was left out when the asking stopped.

The archive keeps the courage and examines the system. A reader who wants only the medals will find them in regimental histories, which are good at medals; a reader who wants to understand why a village emptied its young men will find the answer in a system, and the system is what this chapter has tried to describe.

Where that system carried people is the subject of the final chapter.

Keeping both halves in view is the only way to write about the connection without either celebrating it into a myth or explaining it away, and the sources now exist to do it properly.

Both halves need the other to be true: the courage is real, and the system that rewarded and used it is real, and a book that drops either has stopped describing the past.
