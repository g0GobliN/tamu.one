## [en] Gurung is not Gurkha

Gurung or Tamu describes a community identity; Gurkha belongs to a wider military history involving more than one community. Macfarlane and Indrabahadur Gurung describe Gurungs as one of the major groups from which Gurkha soldiers were recruited, not the only group.

Getting that distinction right matters before any of the history is told.

## [en] Before the Gurkhas: hill soldiers of the eighteenth century

The Gurkha story begins before the British. The army of Gorkha in the eighteenth century was built from the hill communities of its own territory, and Prithvi Narayan Shah’s campaigns relied on soldiers raised through local chiefs and households. Documentary research by Mahesh Chandra Regmi shows that the early Gorkha state recruited mainly Magars and Khasas, and that Gurungs from Lamjung entered the army more gradually, especially as the kingdom absorbed the territories in which they lived. Service was tied to land, status and obligation rather than to a wage contract in the modern sense. When the British later described Gurungs as a “martial race”, they were codifying a relationship that had already existed between hill communities and states for generations.

## [ne] गोर्खाअघि: अठारौँ शताब्दीका पहाडी सिपाही

गोर्खा कथा बेलायतभन्दा अघि सुरु हुन्छ। अठारौँ शताब्दीमा गोर्खाको सेना आफ्नै भूभागका पहाडी समुदायबाट बन्थ्यो, र Prithvi Narayan Shah का अभियान स्थानीय मुखिया र घरपरिवारमार्फत भर्ती भएका सिपाहीमा टेक्थे। Mahesh Chandra Regmi को अभिलेख-अनुसंधानले सुरुको गोर्खा राज्यले मुख्यतः मगर र खस भर्ती गरेको, र लमजुङका गुरुङ सेनामा क्रमशः आएको देखाउँछ, विशेषगरी राज्यले तिनको बसोबास क्षेत्र गाभ्दै जाँदा। सेवा जग्गा, हैसियत र दायित्वसँग जोडिएको थियो, आधुनिक अर्थको ज्याला-सम्झौतासँग होइन। पछि बेलायतले गुरुङलाई “योद्धा जाति” भन्दा त्यो सम्बन्ध नै संहिताबद्ध गरिरहेको थियो, जो पहाडी समुदाय र राज्यबीच पुस्तौँदेखि थियो।

## [en] The regiments and the recruiters

After the Anglo-Nepal War of 1814–16 and the Treaty of Sugauli, the British began recruiting Nepali soldiers, at first cautiously and then systematically. The Gurkha regiments developed their own depots, recruiting officers, language training and regimental identities, and particular districts became associated with particular regiments. Recruiting officers travelled to the hills, examined recruits, recorded their caste and clan, and wrote the descriptions that later became the handbooks. The system was never simply military: it was an economic relationship, a diplomatic instrument and a racial theory at once. For a hill family, the recruiter’s visit was an opportunity, a risk and a judgement about whether a son would return.

## [en] Pension, village and family

Military pay and pensions changed the material life of the hills more than any single development project. A pension was a reliable cash income in an economy otherwise based on grain and barter; it paid for iron roofs, schools, land and ceremonies, and it gave retired soldiers a status that carried into village politics. The absence of men for years at a time changed household labour, leaving women to manage farms and decisions, and it created a culture in which service was expected of sons. Regimental welfare funds, pensions offices and veterans’ associations became part of village life, and the Gurkha connection produced both prosperity and dependence.

## [ne] पेन्सन, गाउँ र परिवार

सैनिक तलब र पेन्सनले पहाडको भौतिक जीवन कुनै पनि विकास आयोजनाभन्दा बढी बदल्यो। पेन्सन अन्न र वस्तु-साटासाटमा आधारित अर्थतन्त्रमा भरपर्दो नगद आय थियो; यसले छानाको फलाम, विद्यालय, जग्गा र विधि तिर्यो, र अवकाशप्राप्त सिपाहीलाई गाउँको राजनीतिमा सर्ने हैसियत दियो। वर्षौँ पुरुषको अनुपस्थितिले घरायसी श्रम बदल्यो, खेती र निर्णय महिलाको हातमा छोड्यो, र छोराबाट सेवा अपेक्षा गर्ने संस्कृति बनायो। रेजिमेन्टल कल्याण कोष, पेन्सन कार्यालय र भेटेरन संस्था गाउँको जीवनको अंश बने, र गोर्खा जोडले समृद्धि र निर्भरता दुवै ल्यायो।

## [en] Memory and myth

The Gurkha story is genuinely important and genuinely partial. Victoria Crosses, campaign histories and regimental memorials record real courage, and families are right to be proud of them. But the same story has been used to define Gurungs entirely as soldiers, to flatten a society of farmers, herders, weavers, priests and traders into a “martial race”, and to make military service seem like the natural destiny of hill men rather than one option among several. Recent scholarship, including Mitra Pariyar’s work on caste, military and migration, treats the Gurkha connection as a social and economic system that shaped everything from marriage to housing. The archive keeps the courage and examines the system.
