## [en] An oral epic of movement

Evans's archaeological and ethnohistorical study includes a translation of the Lẽmakõ Rõh Pye, an oral epic that narrates movement into the region from a distant north.

Read as literature, it is a remarkable thing: a long narrative of departure, journey and settlement, carried in a fixed form by specialists, naming places that the audience knows. Read as history, it has to be handled carefully, because it is an attributed ancestral narrative and not an independently dated itinerary. It says where the community believes it came from, in the order and the language in which that memory is kept.

Those two readings are not in competition. The epic is evidence of what the community remembers, and that memory is itself a historical fact — a fact about how a people understood its own place in the world, and about which places were important enough to be named in a poem that had to be memorised. But it will mislead anyone who treats it as a route map. The places in it are real to the people who recite it, and some can be matched to the landscape; others cannot, and the poem is not obliged to be consistent about distance or duration.

Other journeys in the record are not recited at all. They are walked.

The epic is performed, not read, and performance shapes what it keeps. A poem that must be recited from memory holds what can be remembered in verse — place names, turns in the journey, the names of ancestors — and drops what cannot. That is why its geography is vivid and its chronology vague, and why the places in it are the kind of place a listener would recognise.

## [en] Walking sacred landscapes

Pettigrew examines a ritual soul journey and the ways it was physically retraced and brought into wider discussions of Tamu history.

The route is not a travel itinerary. It is a sequence of places with meanings attached: where the soul goes after death, what it meets on the way, which ancestors are encountered at which point. In the rite, the specialist names the stages so that the soul can find its road, and the geography of that road is the community's older landscape — the ridges, rivers and passes that lie between the living village and the land of the dead.

Walking it again turns an abstraction into an argument. A community that can say "we walked the route our grandparents described" has demonstrated continuity in a way no document could, and the walk becomes evidence in debates about history and identity. It also produces knowledge of a different kind from a book: the distance is felt, the climb is felt, and the places named in the chant are seen.

The uncertainty those routes leave behind has to be stated plainly. A route walked today is a route walked today; it does not date the chant, and it does not prove that the ancestors took that path. What it proves is that the path still means something.

The soul journey and the migration epic share a geography, and that is not a coincidence. Both describe a route from the living world towards an older one, and both use the same landmarks. Walking the ritual route is therefore also a way of walking the migration, which is one reason the walk carries so much argumentative weight for the people who do it.

## [en] Mapping uncertainty

No map in this collection plots a definitive migration line. The exact starting points, dates and path of ancient population movements remain unresolved.

That is a modest conclusion for a chapter about migration, and it is the right one. A map with an arrow on it would be a claim about evidence that does not exist, and arrows have a way of becoming facts once they are printed. The rule this archive follows is that a route may only be drawn where there is an attributed source, a proposed period and an explicit statement of uncertainty, so that a reader can see what is being claimed and by whom.

What can be described with confidence is not the route but the country it crossed, and that country explains a great deal about how the movement happened. Terrain sets the options. People move along valleys, through passes, to pastures, and towards markets, and the shape of the hills decides which of those is possible in a given generation.

Maps of migration are politically loaded in Nepal, because a group's claim to a place can rest on having arrived first. A published arrow is treated as proof of priority, and priority is treated as proof of right. That is precisely why this archive declines to draw one: the absence of an arrow is not a statement that nobody moved, but a refusal to manufacture a claim that would then be used in an argument it cannot settle.

## [en] Routes through the passes

Movement in the Gurung past ran along a few corridors.

The Kali Gandaki and Marsyangdi valleys connected the Tibetan plateau to the middle hills and the plains beyond. The passes north of Manang and Mustang carried salt, grain, wool and pilgrims. The ridges between the valleys carried herds to summer pastures and families to new fields.

A move from one valley to the next could be a day's walk or a generation's work, and it rarely meant abandoning the old place. Households kept land, graves and shrine obligations in one village while living in another. A man might farm one valley, trade in a second and marry into a third, and his children might grow up knowing all three as home. Gurung settlement therefore looks less like a spreading wave than like a network of connected places, each with a story about who came from where and which family stayed behind.

That shape has consequences for how the past should be read. In a network, movement is normal and settlement is provisional; the interesting question is not who arrived first but who kept which connection. It also explains why the same clan can be found in valleys that are days apart, and why two villages may tell different stories about the same ancestor.

The salt trade is the clearest example of how a corridor works. Salt came south from the plateau, grain went north, and the men who carried it knew every pass, shelter and toll point on the way. A trade route is also a marriage route and a labour route, because the people who move goods are the people who meet each other, and the villages along the way become connected in ways that outlast the trade itself.

The passes are the reason the community's map looks the way it does. A pass is a bottle neck: everyone who moves between two regions goes through the same few weeks of weather, the same shelter, the same toll. Villages at the foot of a pass therefore became mixed, multilingual and used to strangers, and the families there often have kin on both sides of the range.

## [en] Why families moved

Families rarely moved for romantic reasons.

Land ran out as households divided inheritances: each generation split the fields, and by the third or fourth the share was too small to feed a family. Epidemics and landslides destroyed villages outright. Taxes, labour demands and warfare pushed people out, and military service, trade and later employment pulled them towards towns, India, Britain and the Gulf. Marriage moved women between villages and sometimes between countries.

Each of these has a different rhythm: the slow pressure of inheritance, the sudden shock of disaster, the planned move of a soldier's family. The community's history is the sum of them. The same family may have moved four times in a century for four different reasons, and the story it tells about itself usually picks the most dignified one — the famine becomes "the year the land failed", the flight from a tax becomes "the year we came to this valley".

That is not dishonesty. It is how families make a history usable, and a reader who wants the reasons rather than the story has to look at the records.

Inheritance is the quietest of the pressures and the most relentless. A holding that feeds one family feeds four poorly when divided among four sons, and the fourth son has to leave or starve. Multiply that by two generations and a village's land is fragmented into strips too small to plough, which is why emigration from the hills is not a recent response to modern life but an old response to arithmetic.

The hills also had a way of absorbing a family that left. A household arriving in a new valley could clear land, join a clan through marriage and be accepted within a generation, which meant that movement did not produce strangers so much as new neighbours. That is one reason the community's memory of migration is often a memory of settlement rather than of loss.

## [en] Measuring a migration

Migration is easy to describe and hard to measure.

Census data records where people are, not where they came from. Remittance figures record money, not movement. Oral accounts record what people remember, which is not the same as what happened, and they change as the people who remember change.

Historians therefore triangulate. Land records show who held fields in which village, and a change of holder can date a departure. Military records show recruitment by district, which tells you where young men were and when. Missionary and travel accounts describe settlements at particular moments, with the prejudices of their authors. Family genealogies, checked against each other, suggest when a branch left and who stayed.

The picture that emerges is of continuous, local, often circular movement rather than a single migration, and of a community that has been mobile for as long as it has been settled. That is a less dramatic story than the epic, and it is the one the records support. One family's version of that movement — and the village where it settled — is the subject of the next chapter.

Triangulation works because the sources fail in different directions. A land record can be wrong about who actually farmed the field; a military record can be wrong about a recruit's age; a genealogy can be wrong about a generation. When three of them agree on a date, the agreement is worth something, and when they disagree the disagreement tells you which one is being polished.

A single example shows how the triangulation works in practice. A land record shows a field changing hands between two named men in a stated year; a military record shows one of those men attested in a regiment three years later; a genealogy records that his son settled in a different valley. Each source alone is thin. Together they date a departure, and the departure can then be compared with an oral account that describes the same move as a flight from a landslide.
