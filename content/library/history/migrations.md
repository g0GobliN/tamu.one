## [en] Routes through the passes

Movement in the Gurung past ran along a few corridors. The Kali Gandaki and Marsyangdi valleys connected the Tibetan plateau to the middle hills and the plains beyond; the passes north of Manang and Mustang carried salt, grain, wool and pilgrims; the ridges between the valleys carried herds to summer pastures and families to new fields. A move from one valley to the next could be a day’s walk or a generation’s work, and it rarely meant abandoning the old place: households kept land, graves and shrine obligations in one village while living in another. This is why Gurung settlement looks less like a spreading wave than like a network of connected places, each with a story about who came from where and which family stayed behind.

## [en] Why families moved

The reasons for migration were rarely romantic. Land ran out as households divided inheritances; epidemics and landslides destroyed villages; taxes, labour demands and warfare pushed people out; military service, trade and later employment pulled them towards towns, India, Britain and the Gulf. Marriage moved women between villages and sometimes between countries. Each of these has a different rhythm — the slow pressure of inheritance, the sudden shock of disaster, the planned move of a soldier’s family — and the community’s history is the sum of them. The same family may have moved four times in a century for four different reasons, and the story it tells about itself usually picks the most dignified one.

## [en] Measuring a migration

Migration is easy to describe and hard to measure. Census data records where people are, not where they came from; remittance figures record money, not movement; oral accounts record what people remember, which is not the same as what happened. Historians therefore triangulate: land records show who held fields in which village; military records show recruitment by district; missionary and travel accounts describe settlements at particular moments; and family genealogies, checked against each other, suggest when a branch left. The picture that emerges is of continuous, local, often circular movement rather than a single migration, and of a community that has been mobile for as long as it has been settled.
