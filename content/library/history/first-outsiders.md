## [en] Before the notebook

For most of their history, Gurungs did not write about themselves in a form that outsiders could read. Knowledge was carried in ritual speech, song, genealogy and the memory of place, and it was transmitted by people who had been trained to hold it. This does not mean the society was without history; it means its history had a different technology. The arrival of writing in Nepali and English created a new kind of record, produced by people who were usually passing through, usually working for a state or an army, and usually asking questions that served their employers. Those records are the oldest written descriptions of Gurungs that survive, and they must be read with that in mind.

## [en] Kirkpatrick and Hamilton

The first sustained British description of Nepal came from Captain William Kirkpatrick, who led an embassy to Kathmandu in 1793 and published an account of the kingdom in 1811. A generation later, Francis Buchanan Hamilton, a physician and surveyor employed by the East India Company, wrote An Account of the Kingdom of Nepal (1819), whose first chapter surveys the country’s peoples. Hamilton lists the Gurungs among the tribes inhabiting the Gorkha territories, alongside Magars, Jariyas, Newars, Murmis, Kiratas, Limbus, Lapchas and Bhotiyas. His information came from informants in Kathmandu and from earlier writers, and it is uneven: useful on crops, dress and customs, unreliable on numbers and origins.

## [ne] Kirkpatrick र Hamilton

नेपालबारे पहिलो निरन्तर बेलायती विवरण क्याप्टेन William Kirkpatrick बाट आयो, जसले १७९३ मा काठमाडौँमा दूतावासको नेतृत्व गरे र सन् १८११ मा राज्यको विवरण प्रकाशित गरे। एक पुस्तापछि East India Company का चिकित्सक तथा सर्वेयर Francis Buchanan Hamilton ले An Account of the Kingdom of Nepal (१८१९) लेखे, जसको पहिलो अध्यायले देशका जनताको सर्वेक्षण गर्छ। Hamilton ले गुरुङलाई गोरखाका क्षेत्रमा बस्ने जातिमध्ये गन्छन् — मगर, जरिया, नेवार, मुर्मी, किरात, लिम्बू, लाप्चा र भोटियासँगै। उनको सूचना काठमाडौँका सूचनादाता र अघिल्ला लेखकबाट आएको थियो, र असमान छ: बाली, पोसाक र चलनमा उपयोगी, सङ्ख्या र उत्पत्तिमा अविश्वसनीय।

## [en] Hodgson’s filing cabinet

The most important early ethnographer of the Himalaya was Brian Houghton Hodgson, British Resident in Kathmandu in the 1830s and 1840s. Hodgson was an obsessive collector of languages, manuscripts, animals and customs, and his papers include a file, now in the British Library as Mss Eur Hodgson/58, described as “ethnography, geography: routes and settlements”, with notes on “customs and manners of Gurungs” dated between 1847 and 1855. Hodgson’s work is the bridge between the envoy’s report and modern ethnography: he was interested in comparison, in language families and in the classification of peoples, and he collected from local scholars rather than only from officials. His papers remain partly unpublished.

## [en] The recruiting officer’s question

From the 1880s a new kind of writer appeared: the Gurkha recruiting officer. Eden Vansittart’s Notes on Gurkhas (1890) grew into Notes on Nepal (1896) and then into the official handbooks Gurkhas (1906, 1915), which described the “character”, physique, customs and home districts of each recruiting community. These books are full of detail that is hard to find elsewhere — house types, festival calendars, clan names, the price of sheep — but their purpose was military. They classified men by "martial" qualities, recommended which districts to recruit from, and helped officers manage soldiers' families. They also helped fix the idea, still current outside Nepal, that Gurungs are above all a soldiering people.

The military tradition produced one more important description. **W. Brook Northey and C. J. Morris**, in _The Gurkhas: Their Manners, Customs and Country_ (1928), wrote the fullest interwar account of Gurung social organisation for a British readership, repeating the four-jat and sixteen-jat scheme as settled fact, describing the priests, the festivals, the houses and the marriage rules, and adding the vocabulary of tribe and caste that their generation took for granted. Their book is more systematic than Vansittart's handbooks and more detailed than Hamilton's chapter, and it is still one of the sources that later writers cite — which is exactly why its assumptions travelled so far. The first anthropologist to live in a Gurung village for an extended period was **Bernard Pignède**, in Mohoriya in 1958, and his _Les Gurungs_ (1966) is the bridge between these officers' notebooks and modern scholarship. Between them, the recruiters and the anthropologist created the archive that Gurungs now read, argue with and correct.

Morris** ले _The Gurkhas: Their Manners, Customs and Country_ (१९२८) मा बेलायती पाठकका लागि गुरुङ सामाजिक संगठनको सबैभन्दा पूरा अन्तरयुद्ध-विवरण लेखे — चार जात र सोर्ह जात योजनालाई स्थापित तथ्यझैँ दोहोर्‍याउँदै, पुरोहित, चाड, घर र विवाह-नियम वर्णन गर्दै, र आफ्नो पुस्ताको सहज जनजाति-जात शब्दावली थप्दै। उनको पुस्तक Vansittart का ह्यान्डबुकभन्दा व्यवस्थित र Hamilton को अध्यायभन्दा विस्तृत छ, र आज पनि पछिका लेखकले उद्धृत गर्ने स्रोतमध्ये एक हो — यसैले त्यसका धारणा यति टाढा फैलिए। गुरुङ गाउँमा लामो समय बसेर अध्ययन गर्ने पहिलो नृवंशविद् **Bernard Pignede** थिए, सन् १९५८ मा मोहोरियामा, र उनको _Les Gurungs_ (१९६६) ती अधिकृतका नोटबुक र आधुनिक विद्वत्ताबीचको पुल हो। दुवै मिलेर — भर्ती अधिकारी र नृवंशविद्ले — त्यो अभिलेख बनाए जो गुरुङले अहिले पढ्छन्, विवाद गर्छन् र सुधार्छन्।

## [en] What they saw clearly

The colonial writers were often good observers of things that could be seen from outside. They describe stone houses with upper grain stores, the great flocks taken to high pastures in summer, the exchange of Tibetan salt for lowland rice, the bangles and necklaces of women, the drums and dances of festivals, and the terraced fields around villages. They record the names of clans and the rules of marriage as they understood them, and they note the annual cycle of work. For a period before photography and before local written records, these descriptions are genuinely valuable, and much of what later anthropologists analysed can be found in them in embryo.

## [en] What they could not see

They could not see much of what mattered most. Women’s work, ritual knowledge held by pachyu and klyepri, the texts recited at funerals, the songs of the Rodhi and the ordinary speech of the villages were either invisible to them or recorded as curiosities. They translated local terms through Nepali, and Nepali through their own assumptions, so that a clan became a “tribe” and a ritual specialist a “priest” or a “witch doctor”. They rarely named their informants. And because they wrote for readers in Calcutta and London, they emphasised what made Gurungs useful or picturesque rather than what made them intelligible to themselves.

## [ne] तिनले देख्न नसकेका कुरा

सबैभन्दा महत्त्वपूर्ण धेरै कुरा तिनले देख्न सकेनन्। महिलाको काम, पाच्यु र क्ल्येप्रीसँग रहेको कर्मकाण्डी ज्ञान, अन्त्येष्टिमा गाइने पाठ, रोदीका गीत र गाउँको साधारण बोली तिनलाई अदृश्य थियो वा जिज्ञासाका वस्तुका रूपमा रेकर्ड भयो। तिनले स्थानीय शब्दलाई नेपाली हुँदै, र नेपालीलाई आफ्नै धारणा हुँदै अनुवाद गरे, जसले क्लेलाई “जाति” र कर्मकाण्डी विशेषज्ञलाई “पुरोहित” वा “ढामी-डाक्टर” बनाइदियो। तिनले सूचनादाताको नाम विरलै राखे। र कलकत्ता तथा लन्डनका पाठकका लागि लेखेकाले तिनले गुरुङलाई आफैँलाई बुझिने बनाउनेभन्दा उपयोगी वा रमाइलो देखाउने कुरामा जोड दिए।

## [en] The martial-race lens

The most consequential legacy of these writers was the martial-race idea. By classifying hill communities as naturally warlike, the colonial army created a system that recruited heavily from particular groups, paid them, pensioned them, and tied their families to military service for generations. Mitra Pariyar’s research shows how this system interacted with caste and migration, producing communities in Britain whose identity was shaped as much by the regiment as by the village. The lens distorted as much as it revealed: it made soldiers visible and farmers invisible, and it encouraged later writers to explain Gurung history as a story of fighting rather than a story of land, kinship, language and ritual.

## [en] Reading colonial sources today

The practical lesson is to use these sources for what they are good at and to argue with them everywhere else. A recruiting handbook is excellent evidence for what a British officer believed in 1906 and for details of material life; it is poor evidence for Gurung self-understanding. Whenever possible, colonial descriptions should be set beside community accounts, later ethnography and the pye. Most of the originals are now digitised: Hamilton’s Account is on Project Gutenberg, Vansittart’s Notes on Nepal is on the Internet Archive, and Hodgson’s papers are catalogued at the British Library. Reading them directly, with their assumptions intact, is more honest than quoting them second-hand.
